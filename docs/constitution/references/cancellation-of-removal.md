## 1. LPR Cancellation of Removal — INA § 240A(a), 8 U.S.C. § 1229b(a)

### Eligibility Requirements (all must be met):
1. **Lawful permanent resident** status for at least 5 years
2. **Continuous residence** in the U.S. for at least 7 years after admission in any status
3. **No aggravated felony** conviction

### Key Definitions:
- **Continuous residence** is broken by a single absence of 90+ days or aggregate absences of 180+ days. INA § 240A(d)(2).
- **Stop-time rule:** Continuous residence stops accruing when a Notice to Appear (NTA) is served OR when the alien commits certain criminal offenses. INA § 240A(d)(1). See *Pereira v. Sessions*, 138 S. Ct. 2105 (2018) and *Niz-Chavez v. Garland*, 141 S. Ct. 1474 (2021) regarding NTA requirements for stop-time.
- **Aggravated felony** is defined at INA § 101(a)(43). This is a term of art — the offense need not be "aggravated" or a "felony" in the common sense. Always check the specific subsection.

### Discretionary Factors:
LPR cancellation is discretionary. Favorable and adverse factors are weighed under the framework of *Matter of Marin*, 16 I&N Dec. 581 (BIA 1978), as refined by subsequent decisions.

## 2. Non-LPR Cancellation of Removal — INA § 240A(b), 8 U.S.C. § 1229b(b)

### Eligibility Requirements (all must be met):
1. **Physical presence** in the U.S. for a continuous period of at least 10 years
2. **Good moral character** during the 10-year period
3. **No disqualifying convictions** (certain criminal offenses, including those listed in INA §§ 212(a)(2), 237(a)(2), 237(a)(3))
4. Removal would result in **exceptional and extremely unusual hardship** to a qualifying relative (U.S. citizen or LPR spouse, parent, or child)

### Key Definitions:
- **Physical presence** is broken by a single absence of 90+ days or aggregate absences of 180+ days. INA § 240A(d)(2).
- **Stop-time rule** applies the same as for LPR cancellation.
- **Qualifying relative** is limited to the applicant's U.S. citizen or LPR spouse, parent, or child. Hardship to the applicant themselves does not count. Hardship to non-qualifying relatives (e.g., U.S. citizen siblings, grandparents) is only relevant insofar as it affects a qualifying relative.
- **Annual cap:** A maximum of 4,000 grants of non-LPR cancellation per fiscal year. INA § 240A(e).

## 3. Key BIA Precedent

### Hardship Standard
- *Matter of Recinas*, 23 I&N Dec. 467 (BIA 2002) — "Exceptional and extremely unusual hardship" means hardship that is substantially beyond what would normally be expected from deportation. Must be to the qualifying relative, not the applicant.
- *Matter of Gonzalez Recinas*, 23 I&N Dec. 467 (BIA 2002) — Found exceptional hardship where single mother of six (four USC children) with no family in Mexico would face extreme difficulty.
- *Matter of Andazola-Rivas*, 23 I&N Dec. 319 (BIA 2002) — Denied cancellation where hardship, while real, did not rise above what is normally expected in removal cases.

### Stop-Time Rule
- *Matter of Pereira*, 26 I&N Dec. 845 (BIA 2016) — Addressed stop-time rule prior to Supreme Court decision in *Pereira v. Sessions*.

### Good Moral Character
- *Matter of Ortega-Cabrera*, 23 I&N Dec. 793 (BIA 2005) — Good moral character analysis.

## 4. Sixth Circuit Key Precedent

### Stop-Time Rule
- *Niz-Chavez v. Garland*, 141 S. Ct. 1474 (2021) — Supreme Court held that a single document must contain all required NTA information to trigger the stop-time rule. A two-document NTA does not stop the accrual of continuous presence. **This is critical in the Sixth Circuit — always check whether the NTA was served in a single document.**
- *Pereira v. Sessions*, 138 S. Ct. 2105 (2018) — An NTA that omits the time and place of the hearing does not trigger the stop-time rule.

### Hardship
- The Sixth Circuit reviews the BIA's hardship determination under the substantial evidence standard and has generally deferred to the BIA's assessment of whether hardship rises to the "exceptional and extremely unusual" level.

## 5. Hardship Analysis — Factors to Develop

When building a hardship argument for non-LPR cancellation, develop evidence on these factors for each qualifying relative:

### Health
- Medical conditions of qualifying relative that require treatment unavailable or inadequate in the home country
- Mental health impact on qualifying relative (especially children)
- Disability or special needs

### Education
- Qualifying relative children's enrollment in school, grade level, language abilities
- Special education needs or services
- Disruption to academic progress

### Economic
- Family's financial dependence on the applicant
- Earning capacity in the home country vs. U.S.
- Property, assets, and financial ties to the U.S.
- Country conditions affecting economic opportunity

### Country Conditions
- Safety and security in the home country
- Conditions specifically affecting the qualifying relative (not the applicant)
- Access to healthcare, education, and basic services

### Family and Community Ties
- Length of qualifying relative's residence in the U.S.
- Integration into community (church, school, organizations)
- Separation from other family members

**Practice note:** The hardship must be to the qualifying relative, not the applicant. Frame every argument through the lens of impact on the USC/LPR spouse, parent, or child. The most successful cases combine multiple hardship factors that together create a cumulative picture exceeding the normal consequences of removal.

## 6. Common Issues and Pitfalls

- **Confusing LPR and non-LPR standards.** LPR cancellation has no hardship requirement. Non-LPR cancellation requires "exceptional and extremely unusual hardship." These are fundamentally different analyses.
- **Hardship to non-qualifying relatives.** Hardship to the applicant's U.S. citizen adult children (over 21) counts, but hardship to siblings, grandparents, or other relatives is only relevant as it affects a qualifying relative.
- **Stop-time pitfalls.** After *Niz-Chavez*, always examine the NTA for completeness. A defective NTA may not trigger stop-time, which could make a client eligible who would otherwise fall short.
- **Criminal issues.** Even minor criminal history can destroy good moral character or trigger a disqualifying conviction. Always do a thorough criminal history review before filing.
- **Annual cap.** The 4,000 annual cap on non-LPR cancellation means that even meritorious cases may face a wait. Factor this into client expectations.
- **Burden of proof.** The applicant bears the burden of establishing all elements by a preponderance of the evidence. Corroboration is expected — uncorroborated testimony alone is rarely sufficient for the hardship element.
