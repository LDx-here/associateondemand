# AOS Discretionary Brief — System Guide
## Kingdom Counsel Firm | Immigration Law Practice

**For:** Any system, paralegal, or drafting tool producing AOS discretionary memoranda.  
**Purpose:** This document contains every rule, every legal standard, every writing principle, and every structural instruction needed to produce a filing-quality AOS discretionary brief from scratch. Nothing here requires outside legal knowledge — it is all written in.

---

## Part 1 — What an AOS Discretionary Brief Is

An AOS Discretionary Memorandum is a legal brief submitted to U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) alongside a Form I-485 (Application to Register Permanent Residence or Adjust Status). Its sole purpose is to persuade a USCIS officer to approve the application.

The brief does two things:

1. **Shows the applicant is legally eligible** — under the statute, the officer has no choice but to consider the application.
2. **Argues that approval is warranted** — under the discretionary standard, the officer should approve it because the positive factors outweigh the negative ones.

The brief is addressed to the USCIS officer reviewing the file. It is filed by the attorney on behalf of the applicant. It should read as confident, clear, and factually grounded advocacy — not as a checklist, a form, or a legal treatise.

## Part 2 — The Legal Framework (Everything the System Needs to Know)

This section contains the complete legal doctrine. The system must apply these rules accurately and cite these sources precisely.

### 2.1 The Statute: INA §245(a)

**Full text of the eligibility provision (verbatim):**

> "The status of an alien who was inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States . . . may be adjusted by the Attorney General, in his discretion . . . to that of an alien lawfully admitted for permanent residence if — (1) the alien makes an application for such adjustment, (2) the alien is eligible to receive an immigrant visa and is admissible to the United States for permanent residence, and (3) an immigrant visa is immediately available to him at the time his application is filed."

**In plain English — three requirements:**

| Requirement | What It Means |
|-------------|---------------|
| 1. Application filed | Form I-485 has been submitted |
| 2. Eligible for immigrant visa AND admissible | Has an approved petition (I-130 or I-140); no disqualifying inadmissibility grounds, or inadmissibility has been waived |
| 3. Immigrant visa immediately available | For immediate relatives (spouse/parent/child of adult U.S. citizen), a visa is always immediately available — this requirement is automatically met |

**What "immediate relative" means:** A spouse, parent, or unmarried child under 21 of a U.S. citizen. Immediate relative status is defined at INA §201(b)(2)(A)(i). Because immediate relatives have an automatically available visa, prong 3 is always satisfied for these cases.

**The "discretion" language:** Note that the statute says the Attorney General "may" adjust status — meaning even a fully eligible applicant can be denied if discretion is exercised against them. This is why the brief exists.

### 2.2 The Three Bars Most Relevant to Overstay Cases

**INA §245(c)** lists categories of people who cannot adjust status even if otherwise eligible. For immediate relatives who entered lawfully (with inspection), the §245(c) bars generally do not apply. The brief should confirm this with a clear statement.

**INA §212(a)(9)(B)** — the 3-year and 10-year unlawful presence bars — are triggered only by **departure** from the United States. An applicant who has overstayed but has not departed does not trigger these bars. This is critical: it means adjustment of status is the correct mechanism for overstay cases, and consular processing would actually create the bar that does not currently exist.

This point must be made in the brief's AOS mechanism section. It reframes the overstay not as a disqualifying factor but as a reason why adjustment is the only appropriate pathway.

### 2.3 The Discretionary Standard: Three BIA Cases

These three cases govern every AOS discretionary analysis. The system must cite all three in the Legal Standard section and may cite them again in the Argument section. The verbatim quotes below are confirmed against the official DOJ/EOIR published decisions.

---

**Matter of Arai, 13 I&N Dec. 494, 496 (BIA 1970)**  
*Public PDF:* https://www.justice.gov/sites/default/files/eoir/legacy/2012/08/27/2027.pdf

**What this case stands for:** The baseline rule. In the absence of serious adverse factors, adjustment should be granted as a matter of course.

**Verbatim quote to use:**
> "In the absence of adverse factors, adjustment will ordinarily be granted, still as a matter of discretion."

**Also useful from Arai:**
> "Where adverse factors are present in a given application, it may be necessary for the applicant to offset these by a showing of unusual or even outstanding equities. Generally, favorable factors such as family ties, hardship, length of residence in the United States, etc., will be considered as countervailing factors meriting favorable exercise of administrative discretion."

**How to use this case in the brief:** After the applicant's equities have been presented, return to Arai to close: because no serious adverse factors exist, the baseline rule favors approval. The brief's job is to establish that the applicant falls under the Arai standard — not the elevated Marin standard that applies when serious adverse factors are present.

---

**Matter of Marin, 16 I&N Dec. 581, 584–85 (BIA 1978)**  
*Public PDF:* https://www.justice.gov/sites/default/files/eoir/legacy/2012/08/17/2666.pdf

**What this case stands for:** The balancing test. The officer must weigh positive equities against adverse factors. As adverse factors become more serious, the applicant needs stronger equities.

**Verbatim quote — the balancing test:**
> "The immigration judge must balance the adverse factors evidencing an alien's undesirability as a permanent resident with the social and humane considerations presented in his behalf to determine whether the granting of section 212(c) relief appears in the best interests of this country."

**Verbatim quote — the elevated standard (cite this to show it does NOT apply):**
> "As the negative factors grow more serious, it becomes incumbent upon the applicant to introduce additional offsetting favorable evidence, which in some cases may have to involve unusual or outstanding equities."

**How to use this case in the brief:** In the Legal Standard, establish the balancing test as the framework. Then in the Argument section, use Marin's elevated standard in reverse: because the adverse factors here are limited, the applicant does not need to show "unusual or outstanding equities." The lower Arai baseline applies.

**List of favorable factors Marin recognizes (for the system to draw from):**
- Family ties within the United States
- Residence of long duration in this country (particularly when residency began at a young age)
- Evidence of hardship to the respondent and family if removal occurs
- Service in this country's Armed Forces
- History of employment
- Existence of property or business ties
- Evidence of value and service to the community
- Proof of genuine rehabilitation (if a criminal record exists)
- Other evidence attesting to good character (affidavits from family, friends, responsible community representatives)

**List of adverse factors Marin recognizes:**
- Nature and underlying circumstances of the ground of inadmissibility at issue
- Presence of additional significant violations of immigration laws
- Existence of a criminal record (nature, recency, seriousness)
- Other evidence indicative of bad character or undesirability as a permanent resident

---

**Matter of Patel, 17 I&N Dec. 597 (BIA 1980)**  
*Public PDF:* https://www.justice.gov/sites/default/files/eoir/legacy/2012/08/17/2842.pdf

**What this case stands for:** The burden of proof. Adjustment is not a right — the applicant must affirmatively demonstrate that discretion should be exercised in their favor.

**Verbatim quote:**
> "The grant of an application for adjustment of status under section 245 is a matter of administrative grace. An applicant has the burden of showing that discretion should be exercised in his favor."

**How to use this case in the brief:** Cite it early in the Legal Standard to establish that the brief's purpose is to satisfy that burden. The brief's structure is then the mechanism for satisfying it — each section is a documented demonstration of merit.

---

### 2.4 Two Supporting Cases (Cite Only When Relevant)

**Matter of Mendez-Morales, 21 I&N Dec. 296, 301 (BIA 1996)**  
*Public PDF:* https://www.justice.gov/sites/default/files/eoir/legacy/2014/07/25/3272.pdf

**Use when:** The brief emphasizes close, dependent family ties — not merely the existence of relatives, but the quality and depth of those relationships.

**Verbatim quote:**
> "If the alien has relatives in the United States, the quality of their relationship must be considered in determining the weight to be awarded this equity."

**How to use:** When the brief develops a family section, close with this citation to signal that the quality of the relationship has been established — not just its nominal existence.

---

**Matter of Edwards, 20 I&N Dec. 191, 196 (BIA 1990)**  
*Public PDF:* https://www.justice.gov/sites/default/files/eoir/legacy/2012/08/14/3134.pdf

**Use when:** There is a criminal record or past adverse conduct in the case, and the applicant has demonstrated positive conduct since.

**Verbatim quote:**
> "A clear showing of reformation is not an absolute prerequisite to a favorable exercise of discretion in every section 212(c) application involving an alien with a criminal record; therefore, section 212(c) applications involving convicted aliens must be evaluated on a case-by-case basis, with rehabilitation a factor to be considered in the exercise of discretion."

**How to use:** Cite in the adverse factors section to prevent the officer from treating any prior misconduct as automatically disqualifying.

---

### 2.5 The USCIS Framework: 1 USCIS-PM E.8

*Live source:* https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-1-part-e-chapter-8

The USCIS Policy Manual is the officer's internal instructions. Citing it back in the brief signals that the attorney is applying the officer's own framework, not inventing a new one.

**Most important lines to cite:**

> "Where an immigration benefit is discretionary, meeting the statutory and regulatory requirements alone does not entitle the requestor to the benefit sought."
> — 1 USCIS-PM E.8(A)

> "A matter of administrative grace where the applicant has the burden of showing that discretion should be exercised in his or her favor."
> — 1 USCIS-PM E.8(B)(1) (quoting Matter of Patel)

**The PM's factor list (mirrors Marin — use both citations together):**

Favorable factors the PM directs officers to consider:
- Ties to family members in the United States and the closeness of the underlying relationships
- Hardship due to an adverse decision
- Value and service to the community
- Length of lawful residence and age at which residency began
- Service in the U.S. armed forces
- History of employment
- Property or business ties
- History of taxes paid
- Evidence of respect for law and order, good character, intent to hold family responsibilities
- Criminal history and whether the applicant has rehabilitated and reformed
- Community service beyond any imposed by courts

Adverse factors the PM directs officers to consider:
- Nature and underlying circumstances of any inadmissibility grounds
- Seriousness of violations and eligibility for waivers
- Criminal history
- Fraud or false testimony in dealings with USCIS or any government agency
- Immigration violations
- Prior removal orders

**Cite the PM** whenever a favorable factor appears in the brief. Format: *"[Factor] is an enumerated favorable consideration under 1 USCIS-PM E.8(C)(2)."* Then cite the corresponding BIA case.

## Part 3 — The Structure: CREAC Applied to an AOS Brief

CREAC stands for **Conclusion → Rule → Explanation → Application → Conclusion.**

It is the foundational structure of legal analysis. In a persuasive immigration brief, CREAC works as follows:

| CREAC Element | What It Does | Where It Lives in the Brief |
|---------------|-------------|----------------------------|
| **Conclusion** | States the outcome the brief seeks | Cover page subtitle; first paragraph of Argument |
| **Rule** | States the legal standard governing the outcome | Section I: Legal Standard |
| **Explanation** | Explains how the rule has been applied in analogous cases | Section I (case law) + Section III intro |
| **Application** | Applies the rule to the client's specific facts | Section III: Argument (Sections A–D) |
| **Conclusion** | Restates the outcome with the argument behind it | Section III-E: Balancing + Section IV: Conclusion |

This structure runs through the entire brief. It also runs inside each individual argument section — each subsection has its own mini-CREAC.

### 3.1 The Full Brief Outline

```
[COVER PAGE]
    Applicant name, A-number
    Subject line: "Memorandum in Support of Application for Adjustment of Status"
    Subtitle: [Case theme — one sentence, attorney-authored]
    Date, prepared by, for submission with Form I-485

I.   LEGAL STANDARD
     A. Statutory Framework (INA §245(a) — the three-prong test)
     B. The Discretionary Standard (Patel → Marin → Arai)
     C. USCIS Policy Framework (1 USCIS-PM E.8 + PM-602-0199 if applicable)

II.  STATUTORY ELIGIBILITY
     A. [Applicant] Was Inspected and Admitted
     B. [Applicant] Is Eligible to Receive an Immigrant Visa
        (approved I-130; immediate relative relationship; visa immediately available)
     C. [Applicant] Is Admissible to the United States for Permanent Residence
        (address any admissibility issues or bars here; confirm none if clean)

III. ARGUMENT: A FAVORABLE EXERCISE OF DISCRETION IS WARRANTED
     [Opening: inject case theme; cite Patel to frame the burden]
     [Theme sentence: "The question before this officer is not whether [applicant] is eligible — 
      she is. The question is whether discretion should be exercised favorably. The answer is yes."]

     A. [PRIMARY EQUITY HEADING — argument claim, not category label]
        [Most compelling equity — developed in 2-4 substantive paragraphs]
        [Cite Marin favorable factor + PM factor + Mendez-Morales if family ties]

     B. [SECONDARY EQUITY HEADING — argument claim]
        [Bundle 2-4 supporting equities here]
        [Each equity: state the fact → develop the significance → cite legal authority]

     C. [AOS MECHANISM HEADING — if post-May 2026 memo or overstay case]
        "Congress Created Adjustment of Status to Permit Eligible Applicants
         to Complete the Immigration Process Without Needless Family Separation"
        [Cite INA §212(a)(9)(B) — departure triggers the bar, not continued presence]
        [Argue AOS advances INA's family-unification purpose]

     D. [ADVERSE FACTORS HEADING — framed as proportionality, not confession]
        "The Circumstances of [Applicant]'s Continued Presence Do Not Diminish
         the Strength of This Application"
        [Acknowledge the adverse fact honestly and directly]
        [Provide context — how it arose, what it reflects]
        [Establish it does not rise to the level requiring outstanding equities]
        [Cite Edwards if rehabilitative conduct is relevant]

     E. [BALANCING]
        "The Balance of Equities Strongly Favors a Favorable Exercise of Discretion"
        [Name the adverse factor plainly — one sentence]
        [Inventory the positive equities in tight declarative form]
        [Apply Arai: because adverse factors are not serious, baseline rule applies]
        [Close with theme sentence: "This is not a case about [adverse factor].
         It is a case about [theme]. A favorable exercise of discretion is warranted."]

IV.  CONCLUSION
     [Restate the ask: favorable exercise of discretion, approval of the I-485]
     [Restate who the applicant is — one sentence from the theme]
     [Close with a confident declarative sentence]

[PAGE BREAK]

FOOTNOTES
     [All citations collected here, numbered, deduplicated]
```

## Part 4 — Fixed vs. Variable Elements

This is the core of the template system. Some content is the same in every brief. Some content is unique to each case. The system must distinguish between them.

### 4.1 FIXED — Never Changes

These passages can be copied verbatim into every AOS brief. They require no modification.

---

**LEGAL STANDARD: The Discretionary Framework**

> Section 245 of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) provides that the Attorney General may, in his discretion, adjust the status of an eligible alien to that of a lawful permanent resident. INA §245(a), 8 U.S.C. §1255(a). Adjustment of status is "a matter of administrative grace," and "[a]n applicant has the burden of showing that discretion should be exercised in his favor." Matter of Patel, 17 I&N Dec. 597 (BIA 1980).
>
> The discretionary analysis requires "a balancing of the negative factors evidencing the person's undesirability as a permanent resident with the social and humane considerations presented on his or her behalf to determine whether relief appears in the best interests of this country." Matter of Marin, 16 I&N Dec. 581, 584 (BIA 1978). Where adverse factors are present, the applicant must present offsetting equities. However, "in the absence of adverse factors, adjustment will ordinarily be granted, still as a matter of discretion." Matter of Arai, 13 I&N Dec. 494, 496 (BIA 1970). The elevated standard requiring "unusual or outstanding equities" is triggered only where adverse factors are serious — where, as here, they are limited, the Arai baseline governs. See Matter of Marin, 16 I&N Dec. at 585.
>
> Meeting the statutory and regulatory requirements alone does not entitle the requestor to the benefit sought. 1 USCIS-PM E.8(A). The burden rests with the applicant to affirmatively demonstrate that a favorable exercise of discretion is warranted. Matter of Patel, supra. This Memorandum satisfies that burden.

*Footnote for this section: Cite Patel, Marin, Arai, and 1 USCIS-PM E.8.*

---

**AOS MECHANISM SECTION: The Congressional Purpose Argument**

*(Use in all cases involving overstays or where the May 2026 PM is relevant.)*

> Congress created adjustment of status to permit eligible applicants who were lawfully admitted to the United States to complete the immigration process without the family separation, risk, and disruption that departure and consular processing would entail. Adjustment of status is not a loophole — it is the mechanism Congress provided for exactly this situation. INA §245(a).
>
> Consular processing would impose consequences that adjustment of status was designed to avoid. Departure from the United States to pursue a visa at a U.S. consulate abroad would trigger the three-year or ten-year unlawful presence bars under INA §212(a)(9)(B) — bars that do not currently apply to [APPLICANT], who has remained in the United States continuously since her lawful admission. Adjustment of status allows the family-unification purpose of the INA to be realized without subjecting [APPLICANT] to those consequences.
>
> A favorable exercise of discretion here advances, rather than frustrates, the policies the immigration laws are designed to protect.

*Footnote: Cite INA §212(a)(9)(B); INA §245(a); any applicable PM.*

---

**BALANCING SECTION CLOSING — The Template Sentence**

> This is not a case about [ADVERSE FACTOR]. It is a case about [CASE THEME RESTATED]. [APPLICANT]'s positive equities — [SHORT INVENTORY] — substantially outweigh the limited adverse circumstance at issue. Under Matter of Arai, a favorable exercise of discretion is the appropriate outcome where adverse factors do not rise to a level requiring unusual equities, and this is such a case. A favorable exercise of discretion is both legally supported and compelled by the facts of this record.

---

**CONCLUSION — Fixed Template**

> For the foregoing reasons, [APPLICANT] respectfully requests that U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services exercise its discretion favorably and approve the pending Application to Register Permanent Residence or Adjust Status (Form I-485).
>
> [APPLICANT] has demonstrated both legal eligibility and compelling grounds for a favorable exercise of discretion. [ONE-SENTENCE RESTATEMENT OF THEME]. A favorable exercise of discretion is warranted.
>

>
> [Attorney name and signature block]

## Part 5 — The Case Theme: The Most Important Input

The case theme is the single most important element in the brief. It cannot be generated by a system from facts alone — it requires legal judgment. However, the system can prompt the attorney to produce it and then use it correctly.

### 5.1 What the Case Theme Is

The case theme is a single sentence that answers the question: **If this officer remembered only one thing about this application, what should it be?**

It is not a legal conclusion. It is not a summary of the brief. It is a **factual frame** — a description of the applicant that makes the officer care about the outcome before they read the first legal argument.

**The template sentence structure:**

> "This case concerns a [description of applicant] whose [most compelling equity] [stakes if denied]."

**Or:**

> "This application presents a [description of applicant] of [character] whose [relationship or role] [depends on / would be severed by / uniquely requires] [applicant's continued presence]."

### 5.2 What Makes a Good Case Theme

A good case theme has three qualities:

1. **Specific** — It is about this person, not every applicant. It uses concrete facts, not abstract categories.
2. **Factual** — It states a fact, not a legal conclusion. "Grandmother of an autistic U.S. citizen child" is a fact. "Deserving applicant" is not.
3. **Stake** — It implies what is lost if the application is denied. "Uniquely qualified to care for her autistic grandson" implies that denial severs that care.

**Weak theme (too generic):**
> "Ms. Sakkhi is a long-time resident with strong family ties and good moral character."

**Strong theme (specific, factual, with stakes):**
> "This case concerns a 68-year-old retired nurse of exceptional moral character whose 40-year medical career has rendered her uniquely qualified to care for her autistic U.S. citizen grandson, and whose devotion to her family, her community, and those in need reflects the values the immigration laws were designed to protect."

### 5.3 Where the Theme Appears in the Brief

The case theme appears **four times:**

1. **Cover page** — as a subtitle in italics beneath "Memorandum in Support of Application for Adjustment of Status"
2. **Opening of Section III** — in the first paragraph of the Argument, after the citation to Matter of Patel
3. **Balancing section (Section III-E)** — in the "This is not a case about X" closing sentence
4. **Conclusion** — as the one-sentence restatement of who the applicant is

## Part 6 — Section-by-Section Writing Instructions

### Section I — Legal Standard

**Purpose:** Establish the rules of the game before presenting the facts.

**What goes here:**
- INA §245(a) — the three-prong eligibility test (one short paragraph)
- The three BIA cases — Patel (burden), Marin (balancing), Arai (baseline) — one paragraph each or combined
- USCIS Policy Manual reference — one sentence confirming the PM's framework mirrors the case law

**Length:** 1–2 pages maximum. This section is not advocacy. It is the factual/legal foundation.

**Tone:** Objective and authoritative. No narrative, no persuasion. Just the law.

**Do not:** Include the client's facts. Do not discuss the applicant at all. Save that for Section III.

**The fixed passage from Part 4.1 above covers this section entirely.** Replace the bracketed references with the specific facts only after Section II.

---

### Section II — Statutory Eligibility

**Purpose:** Confirm that the threshold eligibility requirements are satisfied. This is the CREAC "Rule" step applied to eligibility.

**What goes here:**
- Prong 1: Applicant was inspected and admitted — state entry date, port of entry, visa type
- Prong 2: Eligible for immigrant visa — cite the approved I-130, the immediate relative relationship, and the petitioner's U.S. citizen status; state the relationship
- Prong 2 continued: Admissible — confirm no bars apply; if any admissibility issue exists, address it here with the applicable waiver or exception
- Prong 3: Visa immediately available — one sentence: because [applicant] is an immediate relative of a U.S. citizen, a visa is immediately available as a matter of law pursuant to INA §201(b)(2)(A)(i)

**Length:** Half a page to one page. Clean, factual, efficient.

**Tone:** Factual and definitive. Every sentence should be documentable.

**Do not:** Advocate here. Do not describe the applicant's positive qualities. That goes in Section III.

---

### Section III — Argument

**Purpose:** Persuade. This is the heart of the brief.

**The opening paragraph of Section III (write this first, every time):**

> [APPLICANT] satisfies the statutory eligibility requirements set forth in INA §245(a), as established above. The question before this officer is whether a favorable exercise of discretion is warranted. As established in Matter of Patel, the applicant bears the burden of demonstrating that discretion should be exercised in her favor. [CASE THEME]. The record compiles that demonstration in full.

---

#### Section III-A — Primary Equity Section

**Purpose:** Present the most compelling single equity. This section does the most persuasive work.

**Rule for heading:** The heading must be a claim about this applicant — not a category label.

| Wrong (category) | Right (claim) |
|-----------------|--------------|
| Family Unity | [Applicant]'s Specialized Medical Expertise Is Indispensable to the Care of Her Autistic U.S. Citizen Grandson |
| Humanitarian Concerns | Adjustment of Status Advances the Family-Unification Purposes of the INA and Avoids Severe Disruption to a Vulnerable Child |
| Good Moral Character | [Applicant] Is a Person of Exceptional Moral Character with an Unparalleled Record of Service |

**Structure inside Section A:**
1. State the centerpiece equity — what is it, why does it matter (one paragraph)
2. Develop the specific facts that give it weight (one to two paragraphs)
3. Connect the facts to the legal standard — cite the Marin favorable factor and the PM factor (one paragraph)
4. Close with the Mendez-Morales cite if family ties are the centerpiece

**Length:** 2–4 substantive paragraphs.

---

#### Section III-B — Secondary Equity Section

**Purpose:** Bundle supporting equities. Do not give each minor equity its own section.

**Rule:** If an equity takes fewer than two full paragraphs to develop, it belongs in Section B — not in its own section.

**What typically goes in Section B:**
- Humanitarian factors (age, health, length of separation from family)
- Community service and moral character evidence
- Employment history and financial stability
- Religious community ties
- Educational activities
- Any other positive factors not developed in Section A

**Structure inside Section B:**
1. Introduce the bundle with a sentence connecting it to the theme
2. Develop each equity in 1–3 sentences — fact → significance → authority
3. Cite the applicable Marin factor and PM factor for each
4. Close with a sentence returning to the theme

**Length:** 2–4 paragraphs. Longer if the equities are individually substantial.

---

#### Section III-C — AOS Mechanism Section

**Purpose:** Explain why adjustment of status (rather than consular processing) is the correct pathway, and why USCIS scrutiny of that choice is consistent with approval.

**When to include:** Always include for:
- Cases filed after May 21, 2026 (PM-602-0199 era)
- Cases involving any period of unlawful presence
- Cases where the applicant's travel would trigger or risk triggering a bar

**The fixed passage from Part 4.1 covers most of this section.** Fill in the specific harm that would result from departure in the applicant's case. Examples: 3-year or 10-year bar triggered, separation from dependent minor child, health complications making international travel dangerous, inability to return to care for a disabled or ill family member.

---

#### Section III-D — Adverse Factors Section

**Purpose:** Address the negative honestly, briefly, and in a proportionality frame.

**The heading rule:** The adverse section heading must NEVER use the words "Immigration Violations," "Overstay," or "Unlawful Presence" as a label. The heading must frame the discussion as a proportionality argument.

**Examples:**

| Never Use | Instead Use |
|-----------|-------------|
| "Immigration Violations" | "The Circumstances of Ms. [Name]'s Continued Presence Do Not Diminish the Strength of This Application" |
| "Overstay" as heading | "Ms. [Name]'s Immigration History, Viewed in Context, Reflects Changed Circumstances Rather Than Willful Evasion" |
| "Adverse Factors" | "Any Negative Considerations Are Limited and Do Not Diminish the Applicant's Compelling Equities" |

**Structure inside Section D:**
1. Acknowledge the adverse fact directly — one honest sentence. Do not obscure it.
   > "Ms. [Name] remained in the United States beyond her authorized period of stay."
2. Provide context — how it arose, when, what circumstances led to it
3. Establish what it is not — no criminal conduct, no fraud, no multiple violations, etc.
4. Apply the legal standard — cite Arai to show this level of adverse factor does not trigger the elevated Marin standard
5. Cite Edwards if there is prior criminal history and the applicant has demonstrated rehabilitation

**What not to do:**
- Do not apologize. Do not use words like "unfortunately" or "we acknowledge that."
- Do not lead the officer toward treating the adverse fact as significant by spending too many words on it.
- Do not use "rebuttal" language ("In rebuttal to this adverse factor..."). This is a filing, not an adversarial brief.

**Length:** 1–2 paragraphs. No more.

---

#### Section III-E — Balancing

**Purpose:** Make the comparison explicit. Bring everything together and close with the theme.

**Structure:**
1. Acknowledge the adverse factor in one brief sentence — plainly, without drama
2. State the positive equities in a short, declarative inventory (three to five strong statements)
3. Apply the legal standard — Arai governs; the elevated Marin standard does not apply because adverse factors are not serious
4. Close with the "This is not a case about X" sentence

**The closing sentence template:**
> "This is not a case about [ADVERSE FACTOR, stated flatly]. It is a case about [CASE THEME RESTATED BRIEFLY]. A favorable exercise of discretion is both legally supported and compelled by the facts of this record."

**Length:** 2–3 paragraphs.

---

### Section IV — Conclusion

Use the fixed template from Part 4.1. Fill in the applicant name and restate the theme in one sentence.

---

### Footnotes

**Rules:**
1. All citations go in footnotes only. No in-text parentheticals.
2. The body of the brief reads clean — no citation strings interrupting the prose.
3. Footnotes are numbered consecutively from the beginning of the document.
4. If the same source is cited multiple times, use the same footnote number the second time (deduplication). Do not create a second footnote for the same source.
5. Every verbatim quote must be in a footnote with the exact citation, including the pinpoint page.

**Format for BIA cases:**
> ¹ Matter of Arai, 13 I&N Dec. 494, 496 (BIA 1970).

**Format for USCIS Policy Manual:**
> ² 1 USCIS-PM E.8(A), available at https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-1-part-e-chapter-8.

**Format for statutes:**
> ³ INA §245(a), 8 U.S.C. §1255(a).

## Part 8 — Writing Rules the System Must Follow

These rules govern every sentence in every brief. They are not style preferences — they are the rules that determine whether the brief reads like a filing or a form.

### 8.1 On Structure

1. **The theme appears four times.** Cover subtitle, Section III opening, balancing closing, Conclusion. Every time.
2. **Section headings are argument claims.** If the heading could appear in 500 different briefs, rewrite it.
3. **Minor equities are bundled.** No equity gets its own section if it cannot sustain two full paragraphs.
4. **The adverse section is brief.** One to two paragraphs. It does not get equal space to the positive equities.
5. **The footnote page is separate.** All citations in footnotes, none in the body.

### 8.2 On Language

6. **Never use "Immigration Violations" as a section heading.** Ever.
7. **Never use the word "rebuttal."** This is a filing in support of an application, not an opposition brief.
8. **Never apologize.** Words like "unfortunately," "we acknowledge," and "while it is true that" weaken the brief.
9. **Never use passive voice to obscure facts.** "She remained beyond her authorized stay" is honest. "A period of unlawful presence may have occurred" is evasive and damages credibility.
10. **Use verbatim quotes from sources.** Do not paraphrase the cases or the statute. Use their words.
11. **Cite the PM alongside the case law.** When a favorable factor appears, cite both the relevant BIA case and the corresponding PM provision.

### 8.3 On Facts

12. **Every fact must be documentable.** If a fact is referenced in the brief, a document in the file supports it.
13. **Every bracket must be flagged.** Use [BRACKETS] for any fact that requires attorney confirmation before filing. Do not remove brackets — let the attorney resolve them.
14. **The Mendez-Morales principle applies.** Do not just state that the applicant has family in the U.S. Describe the quality of the relationship — the dependence, the emotional bond, the specific ways the family members rely on each other.

### 8.4 On Tone

15. **Confident, not aggressive.** The brief makes strong claims. It does not challenge or lecture the officer.
16. **Client-centered, not attorney-centered.** The brief is about the applicant's life, not the attorney's arguments. Facts first, law second.
17. **No sycophancy.** Do not begin sentences with "It is respectfully submitted that" or "Counsel respectfully urges." State the conclusion.

## Part 10 — Common Errors and How to Avoid Them

### Error 1: The Legal Standard is too long

**Symptom:** Section I runs more than two pages. It includes extensive case summaries, legislative history, or regulatory analysis.

**Cause:** The system is treating the legal standard as an opportunity to demonstrate legal knowledge rather than to frame the argument efficiently.

**Fix:** The legal standard section has one job — state the rule. Use the fixed passage from Part 4.1. Do not add to it. Do not explain what Marin held in detail. State the test and move to the facts.

---

### Error 2: The argument is organized by factor, not by story

**Symptom:** Section headings are "Family Unity," "Humanitarian Concerns," "Good Moral Character," "Immigration History."

**Cause:** The system used the USCIS factor list as the brief's outline.

**Fix:** The USCIS factor list is the officer's checklist. It is not the brief's structure. The brief's structure comes from the case theme. Ask the attorney what the primary argument is, and build sections around that argument — not around the factor taxonomy.

---

### Error 3: The theme is generic

**Symptom:** The theme sentence sounds like it could describe any applicant: "Ms. [Name] is a long-time resident with strong family ties who has contributed to her community."

**Cause:** The system generated a theme from the factor categories rather than from the most distinctive facts.

**Fix:** The case theme must name what is unique about this applicant. Find the most unusual, most documentable, most emotionally resonant fact in the intake. Build the theme around that specific fact.

---

### Error 4: The adverse section is too long or too apologetic

**Symptom:** Section D runs three or more paragraphs. It includes phrases like "while we acknowledge that" or "it is true that this is a negative factor."

**Cause:** The system over-weighted the adverse factor in an attempt to be "balanced."

**Fix:** The brief is not neutral. It is advocacy. The adverse section acknowledges the fact, provides context, and moves on. Two paragraphs. No apology.

---

### Error 5: Citations are in-text

**Symptom:** The body of the brief contains citation strings like "(Matter of Marin, 16 I&N Dec. 581, 584 (BIA 1978))" after propositions.

**Cause:** The system used standard legal writing citation format.

**Fix:** All citations go in footnotes. The body of the brief must read as clean prose. No parenthetical citations.

---

### Error 6: The balancing section doesn't close with the theme

**Symptom:** The balancing section ends with a generic statement like "For these reasons, a favorable exercise of discretion is warranted."

**Cause:** The system forgot to return to the theme.

**Fix:** The balancing section must close with the "This is not a case about X. It is a case about [THEME]" sentence. Every brief. Every time. This is the most important sentence in the document.

---

## Part 11 — Language Bank: Ready-to-Use Phrases

These phrases have been refined for filing quality. The system may use them directly or adapt them for the specific facts.

### Opening the Argument Section

> "[APPLICANT] satisfies the statutory eligibility requirements set forth in INA §245(a), as established above. The question before this officer is whether a favorable exercise of discretion is warranted. Under Matter of Patel, the applicant bears the burden of demonstrating that discretion should be exercised in her favor. [CASE THEME]. The record compiles that demonstration in full."

### Introducing a Favorable Equity

> "Among the most compelling favorable considerations in this case is [EQUITY]. Under Matter of Marin, [RELEVANT FAVORABLE FACTOR] is among the equities that weigh in favor of a positive exercise of discretion. See also 1 USCIS-PM E.8(C)(2). Here, that equity is not merely present — it is central to the applicant's circumstances."

### Connecting Facts to the Mendez-Morales Quality-of-Relationship Standard

> "The [RELATIONSHIP] between [APPLICANT] and [FAMILY MEMBER] is not nominal. [DESCRIBE THE SPECIFIC, DAILY, DOCUMENTED NATURE OF THE RELATIONSHIP]. As the Board has instructed, 'the quality of [the] relationship must be considered in determining the weight to be awarded this equity.' Matter of Mendez-Morales, 21 I&N Dec. 296, 301 (BIA 1996). That quality is substantial."

### Introducing the AOS Mechanism Section

> "Congress created adjustment of status to permit eligible applicants who were lawfully admitted to the United States to complete the immigration process without needless family separation. INA §245(a). Adjustment of status is not a workaround — it is the pathway Congress designed for this situation."

### Explaining the Unlawful Presence Bar Does Not Apply

> "Notably, the three-year and ten-year unlawful presence bars under INA §212(a)(9)(B) are triggered by departure from the United States — not by continued presence. [APPLICANT], who has remained continuously in the United States since her lawful admission on [DATE], has not triggered these bars. Departure for consular processing would impose precisely the consequence that adjustment of status exists to avoid."

### Opening the Adverse Section

> "[APPLICANT] remained in the United States beyond her authorized period of stay. This fact is acknowledged directly and without minimization. However, it is also the only adverse consideration in this record — and its character, context, and limited scope place it well within the range of adverse factors that the Arai standard addresses without requiring a showing of unusual equities."

### Establishing Limited Weight of the Adverse Factor

> "The Board has established that the elevated standard requiring 'unusual or outstanding equities' is triggered only where adverse factors are serious in nature. Matter of Marin, 16 I&N Dec. at 585. The sole adverse factor here — [DESCRIBE IT] — does not approach that threshold. There is no fraud, no criminal conduct, no pattern of immigration violations, and no prior removal order. The Arai baseline governs."

### Opening the Balancing Section

> "The balance of equities in this case is not close. On the adverse side of the ledger: [ADVERSE FACTOR IN ONE PHRASE]. On the positive side: [INVENTORY OF 3-5 STRONG POSITIVE FACTS IN TIGHT DECLARATIVE FORM]. Under Matter of Arai, a favorable exercise of discretion is ordinarily granted in the absence of serious adverse factors. This is such a case."

### Closing the Balancing Section (The Theme Sentence)

> "This is not a case about [ADVERSE FACTOR, stated plainly]. It is a case about [CASE THEME, restated briefly]. A favorable exercise of discretion is both legally supported and compelled by the facts of this record."
